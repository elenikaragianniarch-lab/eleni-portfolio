Night Walk

A route finder for walking home at night that also shows which streets to fix. It compares the quick way and the safe way across 2,560 simulated walks, finds the streets that make the quick way unsafe, and tests design fixes for them before anything is built.

Year
2026
Role
Individual prototype
Area
King's Cross and Camden, London
Status
Working prototype, runs in the browser

Why I'm building it

I've walked home at night choosing the route the way most women do: which street is lit, which one has people on it, which shortcut to skip. I made a short film about that fear, The Bus. At LOvMe.ai I worked on safety advice and spoke to women about how they move through cities at night. And my thesis, The Unseen City, is about what data about cities leaves out. Night Walk is where the three meet.

The problem

In Britain, 82% of women say they feel unsafe walking alone in a park or open space after dark (ONS, 2022). Map apps send people the quickest way, which is often the dark one. Safety apps send them around it. Neither tells a council which street to fix first, or what fixing it would change.

Public money is already going into this: the Home Office's Safer Streets Fund has put £159 million into 413 local projects, much of it lighting, CCTV and design.

What it found

+1.2 minaverage extra time to walk the safe way
73% → 93%share of the walk on lit streets, quick way against safe way
+87walks that no longer need a detour if the top three streets are fixed
73% → 80%light on the quick way home after those three fixes

Lighting the Regent's Canal towpath and opening up its underpasses does the most: 58 more walks become safe enough and 100 minutes of detours disappear. Camden Street comes next. Some streets that look bad on a map, such as Camden Road, change nothing when fixed, because people already avoid them for other reasons. That's the kind of thing a council can only see with a tool like this.

How it works

  1. Walk. A route finder with safest and shortest routes, the cost in minutes and step-by-step navigation.
  2. See. 2,560 simulated walks between random points show where the quick way and the safe way split.
  3. Diagnose. Every street is scored on light, people around, sightlines, help nearby and incidents.
  4. Fix. Design moves are tested on all 2,560 walks before anything is built.
  5. Propose. Ranked proposals with before and after sections, rough cost and who should act.

Python, NumPy, SciPy, OpenStreetMap, police.uk, Dijkstra routing, minimax-regret negotiation between four walker profiles, vanilla JS and SVG. 8,165 street segments.

Who it could serve

Councils and police and crime commissioners bidding for safety funding, developers and estates planning night-time public space, canal and transport operators, and consultancies writing women's safety audits. The method works for any city with open map data.

Limits

The scores are rules I designed from open data (OpenStreetMap and one month of police.uk records). They haven't yet been tested with the women who walk these streets, lighting is only tagged on 59% of them, and impact is measured on simulated walks rather than real footfall. The next step is walk-alongs, a short survey and council lighting data. It is a design prototype and shouldn't be used as safety advice.
